Based on your six-mouse dataset, these are the most useful directions:

| Idea | What it predicts | How it fits your project |
|---|---|---|
| **Predict tissue-region identity** | Whether a spot belongs to a collagen-associated region, using gene expression | Most feasible extension; connects molecular signatures to spatial biology |

**My recommendation is tissue-region prediction, followed by testing which genes help the model generalize.** It gives you a manageable question:

> Can a small gene-expression signature identify collagen-associated tissue regions in a mouse the model has never seen?

A concrete version would be:

1. **Start with the 48/72-hour samples**, where your collagen-associated annotations are relevant. Keep the distinct 24-hour necrosis context separate.
2. Train a simple, interpretable classifier, such as regularized logistic regression, to predict region membership.
3. Hold out one entire mouse at a time. Randomly splitting spots would let closely related observations from the same mouse enter both training and testing. Grouped cross-validation supports keeping each mouse entirely within one split. [Scikit-learn documentation](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/cross_validation.html#cross-validation-iterators-for-grouped-data)
4. Perform gene selection and scaling using training mice only. Exclude genes used to construct the region labels; even then, agreement measures reproduction of those annotations rather than independent validation of fibrosis.
5. Show **predicted versus annotated spatial maps**, performance for each held-out mouse, and the genes whose contributions remain consistent across mice.

This would give your app an engaging demonstration: select a held-out mouse, display the predicted regions, and examine whether your prioritized targets contribute to the prediction. A predictive gene is still an associated marker; its contribution would not establish that inhibiting it helps.

If you want the predictive component to focus directly on **drug-target usefulness**, perturbation-response modeling is the stronger direction. You would need measured outcomes after gene knockdown or drug treatment, ideally in a relevant liver system. Your current data contain no treatment outcomes from which to learn that prediction.

For the current deadline, I would scope the addition as **“cross-mouse prediction of collagen-associated spatial regions”** and keep therapeutic response prediction as future work. With only four relevant mice, the results would be exploratory, but the prediction task and its evaluation would be concrete.

I see three options:

| Direction | Drug-discovery question | Role of RL | Scope |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drug repurposing** | Which existing compounds oppose the expression pattern associated with injured regions? | Optional experiment-selection layer | Most practical |
| **Adaptive target screening** | Which gene or compound should we test next with a limited budget? | Bandit or sequential decision policy | Best fit for your RL interest |
| **Molecule optimization** | Can we modify compounds to improve predicted target activity and chemical properties? | Policy chooses molecular changes | Largest departure from your project |

**My recommendation is drug repurposing plus an adaptive screening experiment.**

First, extend your spatial analysis into compound hypotheses. Build an expression signature comparing collagen-associated regions with other regions within each mouse, map the genes to human identifiers, and search for compounds with opposing perturbation signatures. Connectivity Map provides experimental compound-response signatures and tools for comparing them with an input signature. [Broad Institute’s CMap resources](https://clue.io/code)

The output would be something like:

> “Compound A opposes this region-associated expression signature, acts on target B, and has these supporting evidence and unresolved liabilities.”

Because you lack healthy controls, opposing that signature would **not establish restoration of healthy tissue**. Cell type, dose, and exposure time in the external experiments would also matter.

Then add an agent that chooses what to test next. Frame the research question as:

> **Under a fixed screening budget, can an adaptive policy discover more experimentally active candidates than our current ranking?**

A concrete setup would be:

| Element | Proposed definition |
|---|---|
| **State** | Candidate features, results revealed so far, and remaining screening budget |
| **Action** | Select an untested gene or compound |
| **Feedback** | Reveal its measured outcome from an external perturbation screen |
| **Reward** | A predefined experimental hit or measured improvement in the screen’s endpoint |
| **Evaluation** | Hits discovered after 10, 20, or 50 tests, compared with random selection and fixed ranking |

You could start with a bandit-style or active-learning policy. These are simpler entry points than deep RL. GeneDisco provides a precedent for benchmarking adaptive experimental selection using drug-discovery perturbation datasets. Dataset suitability would need checking before we commit to it. [GeneDisco paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.11875)

**The crucial requirement is independent feedback.** Rewarding the agent with your existing weighted target score would mostly teach it to optimize that formula. Using held-back experimental measurements lets you test whether adaptation actually improves discovery. A generic screening dataset could demonstrate the method, while liver-specific usefulness would remain a separate question.

This gives the project a coherent progression:

**Spatial evidence → candidate targets and compounds → adaptive selection of experiments → measured discovery performance.**

For your deadline, I’d make compound nomination the core extension and the adaptive screening benchmark a stretch goal. Molecule-generating RL is a legitimate alternative, but it would require a much larger change in data, tooling, and validation.

## My request:
Why is liver injury important

**Codex:** Liver injury matters because the liver does essential jobs: it processes nutrients and medicines, removes harmful substances, and makes proteins needed for blood clotting.

When it is injured, several things can happen:

- **Severe, sudden damage can cause liver failure**, which can be life-threatening.
- **Repeated or lasting injury can cause scarring**, eventually making it harder for the liver to function.

The liver can repair itself, but that repair has limits. Your project explores which genes are involved after injury and which compounds might help reduce damage or support healing. Understanding that distinction matters: blocking a repair process could make things worse.
